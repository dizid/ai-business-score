A few weeks ago I typed "best plumber in my area" into ChatGPT, mostly to
test something in the tool I was building. I expected a list — ten options,
maybe, the way Google would give me. What I got instead was two names and a
sentence about why the first one was a safer bet for an emergency call-out.
That was it. No links to click, no map, no "see all results." Just an
answer, delivered with the confidence of a friend who'd already made up
their mind for me.

I've built websites for small businesses for years. I know exactly how much
work goes into landing on page one of Google — the schema markup, the
review requests, the six months of blog posts nobody reads but Google
apparently does. None of that showed up anywhere in what ChatGPT told me.
The business it picked wasn't the one with the best SEO. It was just the
one the model decided to say out loud.

That's the part that should worry more business owners than it currently
does.

For twenty-five years, "being findable online" meant one thing: rank on the
search results page. Even if you weren't #1, you were still in the room —
position 6, position 9, whatever, a click away. The entire SEO industry
exists because being in the room, even near the back of it, still got you
traffic. People scrolled. People compared.

An AI answer doesn't have a back of the room. It has a foreground and
everyone else. When Gemini or ChatGPT answers "who does X in my city," it's
not handing the user ten options to weigh — it's making the call itself,
usually naming one business, sometimes two or three, and moving on. If
you're not one of those names, you didn't rank low. You didn't rank. You
weren't in the conversation the model had with someone who was, right then,
ready to buy.

I don't think this is a five-years-from-now problem. I think it's already
happening in the background of a lot of purchase decisions, quietly enough
that most business owners have noticed the symptom without noticing the
mechanism — a slow, unexplained dip in the phone ringing that doesn't match
anything in their Google Analytics, because Analytics has nothing to say
about a conversation that happened entirely inside someone else's chat
window.

I ended up building a small tool, Foreground, to actually measure this
instead of guessing at it — it runs the kind of question a real customer
would ask ("best [category] in [city]," that sort of thing) across four
different AI models and checks whether a business gets named, and where.
Not because I think everyone needs a dashboard for this. Mostly because I
got tired of not knowing the answer for my own clients, and "I don't know"
isn't something you can act on.

Here's the uncomfortable part, and it's the reason I'm writing this instead
of just shipping the tool quietly: a business can be doing everything right
by the old rules — solid reviews, a clean site, decent local SEO — and
still be completely invisible in this new front page. The two systems
don't reward the same things. Good SEO gets you found by a crawler. Getting
cited by an AI model means the model already has a clear, specific,
easily-repeated answer for what makes you the one to pick — and most
business websites, if I'm honest, don't actually say that anywhere in plain
language. They say "quality service you can trust," which isn't a sentence
a language model can do anything with.

I'll get into exactly what that means for what to change on your site in
another post. This one is really just to make the case that the front page
moved, and most people are still optimizing for the old one. If your
customers are increasingly asking an AI before they ask Google, the
question worth sitting with isn't "am I ranking." It's: if someone asked
ChatGPT about your category right now, would your name come up at all?

I ran that question against my own site the first time mostly out of
curiosity. I was not thrilled with the answer. That's a decent reason to
check yours before you assume you already know.

— Marc. I build Foreground, which checks exactly this across four AI
