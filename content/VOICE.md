# Marc's Voice — style guide for AI-written content

Extracted 2026-09-19 from Marc's own Google Docs (personal logbook 2026,
an old brainstorm/ideas dump 2013-2014, and one fully-written personal essay)
— not from any existing Foreground marketing copy, which so far has been
entirely AI-drafted and never passed through Marc's own hand (see this
repo's root `CLAUDE.md` on the blog pipeline: "content unreviewed by Marc").
This file is the fix for that gap: a set of mechanical rules for making
future AI-written content actually sound like him, rather than generic
SaaS-marketing voice.

**Deliberately excluded**: the two raw source docs contain a lot of
genuinely private material (health, relationships, family, one very
personal essay). None of that content, or any specific fact from it, is
repeated here — this file only extracts *how he writes*, not *what he
wrote about*. Nothing below should be treated as biographical material to
reuse in copy.

## Core traits

- **Blunt and factual over emotive.** States things plainly, then lets the
  plain statement carry the weight — doesn't editorialize on top of it.
  "That's the whole story really" does more work than three adjectives
  would.
- **Compresses, doesn't pad.** Cuts straight to the point. No throat-clearing
  opener, no "in today's world..." — starts mid-thought or with the fact
  itself.
- **Self-aware, not self-serious.** Comfortable naming his own flaws or
  contradictions in the same breath as a claim ("I know. I know.") instead
  of hiding them. Undercuts anything that starts to sound like a pitch.
- **Zero tolerance for corporate polish.** No "leverage," "unlock," "elevate,"
  "seamless," "robust," "game-changer," "in today's fast-paced world." If a
  phrase could appear in any SaaS's generic landing page, it's wrong for
  this voice.
- **Confident, not salesy.** States what the product does like a fact, not
  a pitch. Doesn't oversell with exclamation marks or superlatives — the
  flat delivery *is* the confidence.

## Sentence & paragraph mechanics

- **Short sentences. Fragments used on purpose.** "That's just the cars."
  "That's enough." A one-line paragraph is a legitimate move for emphasis,
  used sparingly, right after a longer build-up.
- **Lists of concrete specifics over abstractions.** Prefers "twelve car
  crashes" to "a difficult life" — a countable, specific detail always
  beats a vague summary. Applies directly to product copy: prefer specific
  numbers (scan counts, model names, dollar figures) over vague claims
  ("comprehensive," "powerful").
- **Repetition as a rhetorical tool**, not a mistake — repeating a short
  phrase back-to-back for emphasis reads as deliberate, not sloppy.
- **Rhetorical questions used to set up the next line**, then answered
  immediately — not left hanging.
- **Section headers are short and literal** ("The List. As It Is.") — never
  cute or punny for its own sake.
- **Closes with a single short line that restates the core point**, not a
  call-to-action tacked on for its own sake. If there IS a CTA, it stays as
  plain and short as everything before it.

## Vocabulary & diction

- Plain, spoken register — the kind of words used in conversation, not in
  a press release. Short Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones ("cut" not
  "eliminate," "show up" not "materialize").
- Profanity, when it appears, is used the way punctuation is used — for
  emphasis at a specific moment, not as filler or a running bit. In
  customer-facing copy: omit it, but keep the same *directness* that made
  it feel natural in the source.
- Casual contractions and fragments are fine even in fairly serious
  writing — this voice doesn't switch to formal register just because the
  topic is important.

## What to avoid (the default AI-marketing failure mode)

- Hedging ("might help," "could potentially") — state it or cut it.
- Stacked adjectives ("powerful, intuitive, seamless") — pick the one true
  claim and drop the rest.
- Rhetorical throat-clearing openers ("In today's AI-driven world...",
  "As businesses increasingly turn to...").
- Exclamation marks used to manufacture excitement instead of earning it.
- Vague superlatives with no number behind them ("massive impact,"
  "game-changing results").

## Example: generic AI-marketing vs. this voice

Applied to Foreground copy specifically — same claim, two deliveries:

| Generic AI-marketing | This voice |
|---|---|
| "Unlock powerful insights into your AI visibility with our cutting-edge scanning technology!" | "We scan your business against 5 AI models and tell you if you show up. That's it." |
| "In today's AI-driven search landscape, businesses can no longer afford to ignore their AI visibility." | "ChatGPT doesn't send you traffic. It just answers the question. If it doesn't mention you, that customer's gone." |
| "Our comprehensive platform delivers actionable recommendations to elevate your brand's presence." | "You get a score, the competitors AI picked instead of you, and three things to fix first." |

## How to use this file

Before writing any Foreground social post, blog draft, or ad copy: write
the honest, flat version of the claim first (what does it actually do, what
number backs it up), then trim padding and hedging rather than starting
from a "marketing voice" and trying to inject personality into it
afterward. Personality here comes from *subtraction* — cutting the fluff
that a normal marketing draft would include — not from added flair.
